Posture check

Uses your webcam to estimate how far your wrist bends while you type, then compares it to lab-measured pressure thresholds for the carpal tunnel.

How to set it up

  1. Side view measures up and down bend (extension and flexion). Put the camera level with your desk, off to one side, so it sees your elbow, forearm and hand.
  2. Top view measures side bend toward the pinky (ulnar) or thumb (radial). Point the camera down at your keyboard with your forearms in frame.
  3. Hold your wrist straight in line with your forearm and press Calibrate. Then type normally.

What the colors mean

Extension (up)< 27°27–33°> 33°
Flexion (down)< 38°38–49°> 49°

Radial (thumb side)< 18°18–22°> 22°

Caution and high cut-offs are the angles where 75% of people stay below 25 and 30 mmHg of carpal tunnel pressure (Keir et al., 2007). A webcam estimate is approximate, typically within several degrees, so treat it as a coach, not a clinical measurement. Video never leaves your computer.

The research behind WristGuard

What the evidence supports, how strongly, and how it shaped each feature.

1. Wrist angle raises pressure on the median nerve

The median nerve runs through the carpal tunnel with nine finger tendons. Bending the wrist narrows the tunnel and raises pressure inside it. Keir, Bach, Hudes and Rempel (2007) measured pressure across postures and derived angle limits: to keep 75% of people under 30 mmHg, stay within about 33° extension, 49° flexion, 15° ulnar deviation, and 22° radial deviation. Ulnar deviation (bending toward the pinky) has the least room.

Standard keyboards put people close to those limits: Marklin et al. (1999) measured 15–23° of extension and 10–16° of ulnar deviation during normal typing, with the forearm rotated 62–68° palm-down.

In WristGuard: the posture checker's color zones use these exact thresholds.

2. Computer use is a real but modest risk factor

A 2015 meta-analysis (Shiri & Falah-Hassani) found that among office workers, computer use was linked to higher odds of carpal tunnel syndrome (OR 1.34), with mouse use the stronger signal (OR 1.93). The authors call it a minor occupational risk factor. In a large Danish cohort, Andersen et al. (2003) found mouse use above roughly 20 hours a week predicted new tingling and numbness symptoms, while keyboard use did not.

In WristGuard: the timers count active input time, because exposure dose is what the studies track.

3. Breaks: helpful in field studies, uncertain in reviews

In a NIOSH field study of 42 data-entry workers, adding four 5-minute breaks per shift cut forearm, wrist and hand discomfort and eyestrain with no loss in keystrokes (Galinsky et al., 2000). McLean et al. (2001) found microbreaks every 20 minutes reduced discomfort in the wrist, neck, shoulder and back without hurting productivity.

The limits: the 2025 Cochrane review of 9 trials rated the evidence that extra breaks prevent musculoskeletal symptoms as low certainty, with small effects. Breaks are cheap and low-risk, not a cure.

In WristGuard: 20-minute microbreaks and hourly exercise breaks by default, with snooze and idle detection so the tool costs as little attention as possible.

4. Gliding exercises: modest support

A systematic review of 4 randomized trials found tendon and nerve gliding exercises, added to usual care, improved symptom severity and function in carpal tunnel syndrome (Kim, 2015). The evidence is small and mostly about people who already have symptoms.

In WristGuard: tendon glides are in the default routine. The median nerve glide is optional and marked with a caution.

5. Braces while typing can backfire

The 2024 AAOS/ASSH clinical guideline rates bracing as strongly supported for improving carpal tunnel symptoms, and braces are commonly prescribed for night wear. During work the picture changes: in a lab study, wrist splints cut wrist bending but raised shoulder abduction by 22%, and forearm flexor effort climbed when people bent against the splint (Shu & Mirka, 2006). The authors warned splints at work may do more harm than good.

In WristGuard: the tool coaches posture instead of immobilizing the wrist. OSHA advises resting the heel of the palm, not the wrist, on a palm rest, and only between keystrokes.

See a clinician if

  • Numbness or tingling in the thumb, index and middle fingers lasts more than a couple of weeks
  • Symptoms wake you up at night
  • You notice weakness, clumsiness, or dropping things
  • Pain keeps getting worse despite breaks and setup changes
